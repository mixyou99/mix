## (1) Supplement F — grading perspectives

None of the six questions closes on a single answer. Grading looks at **the route to the grounds**, not the conclusion. Below is where the grading separates in each question.

### F-1 · One variable, two statements

The question asks students to render the two definitions quoted in Exhibit A as inequalities.

- **Part 1** — the separation happens at transcription. Were the two terms of the subtraction kept in the manuscript's order, and was the direction of the inequality kept as the manuscript's condition states it? A student who reaches the point that the two quotations attach **different conditions to the same subtraction** has passed part 1. Answers that do not reach it usually rewrite one of the two without noticing; pointing at that spot and handing it back is enough.
- **Part 2** — did the student show that the same two values give **opposite results** under the two definitions? Any numbers will do. The only check is whether the chosen numbers actually separate the two definitions.
- **Part 3** — this is the body of the question. Answering **no** to "can this be settled inside the manuscript" is only half. The student must write concretely **what more would be needed** to settle it. Raw data, a codebook, an author query — any of these is enough. An answer that picks one definition and asserts it is correct gets asked **where the grounds came from**. If they came from outside the manuscript, the grading point is whether the answer knows that.

### F-2 · When a correlation and a coefficient point differently

- **Part 1** — did the student split the two numbers into **two questions** in two sentences? Answers that fold them into one sentence usually stall at the next part.
- **Part 2** — did the student **separate** what the fixed effects remove from what they leave? Naming both the facility's fixed characteristics and the time period is enough. Going further to say what remains as **week-to-week variation inside one facility** is a strong answer.
- **Part 3** — "you cannot use it" is not the whole answer. **Why not** has to follow from the separation made in parts 1 and 2. An answer that gets parts 1 and 2 right and then closes part 3 with the slogan that correlation is not causation loses marks; the slogan does not do the work this question asks for.

### F-3 · Reading a standalone coefficient in a model with an interaction

- **Part 1** — did the student state exactly **at what value of the other variable** the standalone coefficient is the effect? Getting this wrong collapses parts 2 and 3 automatically.
- **Part 2** — the core of the question. It asks whether the condition from part 1 is **an observable state in this data**. Look for whether the student went back to the variable's definition (the response-speed definition in Exhibit A). An answer that writes "observable" or "not observable" without reading the definition has no grounds.
- **Part 3** — the judgment in part 2 must carry part 3. If the two parts run separately, the answer does not go to the top band even with parts 1 and 2 correct.
- **Part 4** — **which side the student picks is not graded.** "Coincidence" and "it means something" are both available. The grading point is the material behind the judgment: did the student note that the two coefficients **measure different things**, and did the student look at the standard errors? Only assertions with no material lose marks.

### F-4 · When the sample count appears as two values

- **Part 1** — did the student transcribe the source of each value from Exhibit A **without omission**? A missing location makes part 2 unstable. Completeness of the list is what is graded.
- **Part 2** — **this is the one part where "it cannot be settled" is the answer.** The reason for it must come out of the list in part 1. Reading that list shows the two values **are not split by stage but mixed across locations**; the check is whether the student got that far. → See (7)(d) alongside this.
- **Part 3** — did the student separate what the manuscript states from what it does not? The stated sentence is quoted in Exhibit A. **An answer that infers past what that sentence covers** does not go to the top band; that is the opposite of the habit this case teaches.
- **Part 4** — the question asks for a reviewer's request **as a sentence**. The check is whether the request is **in a checkable form**. "Please clarify" is not a request. What is to be submitted, and in what form, has to be on the page.

### F-5 · How to report a hypothesis that was not supported

- **Part 1** — did the student decide **not to put the coefficient's sign in the conclusion**, and does the sentence reflect that decision? For answers that keep the sign, check whether they addressed the relation between the coefficient and its standard error.
- **Part 2** — did the student separate "does not support it" from "supports the opposite"? Mixing these collapses part 3.
- **Part 3** — the most important distinction in the whole case. Look for whether the student explained the difference **in terms of the state of the evidence**, rather than restating the two sentences in other words.
- **Part 4** — either choice is fine. The only check is whether **the reason connects to the distinction in part 3**. An answer that grounds the choice in the reporting context — what the reader will go and do — is a strong one.

### F-6 · The limits of a proxy measure

- **Part 1** — did the student write the assumption **as a proposition**? "Review counts reflect bookings" is not an assumption. What relation must hold, and under what condition, has to be on the page.
- **Part 2** — is the situation that breaks the assumption **concrete**, and does it actually break the assumption written in part 1? The two often fail to match.
- **Part 3** — the student has to name one conclusion sentence from this case. Check whether the named sentence really depends on that assumption. **An answer that names nothing and says "the whole thing depends on it" has dodged the question.**

### Running the grading

- **Do not announce per-question weights in advance.** If you do, students count the parts and spread their effort. These questions are built so that a collapsed early part collapses what follows, and equal weighting does not fit that.
- **F-1 and F-4 are a pair** (things not settled inside the manuscript). **F-2 and F-3 are a pair** (a variable whose value differs by where you look). When only one of a pair is right, sending the student back to the other one usually produces a self-correction.
- **F-5 can stand alone.** If a short session allows only one question, use F-5.

## (2) The description column of Table 1 is evidence — the discovery route

The best moment in this case is when a student starts **using a table's description column as evidence**. That column is usually read past; here it separates from the body text.

**What the instructor must not say first** — that the two definitions differ. Say it up front and the student merely confirms it, and will not look at that spot in the next paper they read.

**The order**

1. Open Table 1 in Exhibit E **first**. One task only: each student transcribes the description column of the six variables into one sentence of their own. No body text at this stage.
2. Then open Exhibit A, where the manuscript's body definition is quoted alongside.
3. **Ask**: does the sentence you just wrote say the same thing as the one you are reading now?
4. Wait until they get there. If they do not, narrow it: "Only one of the six differs. Which one?"

**What the instructor should watch** — what a student does after arriving. It usually goes one of three ways.

- They try to settle on one of them → this leads into (7)(a)
- They write both down and move on → the most common reaction in practice; if they stop here, send them back through F-1 part 3 to see that the coefficient's reading stays open
- They ask back which one they should read the coefficient under → the reaction this case aims for

**The same route works for the COVID-case control variable.** Both statements are quoted side by side in Exhibit A, and item (7) of body §9 picks it up. Use it as a second round if there is time, and substitute F-1 if there is not.

## (3) Four things the manuscript does not settle — what each one is material for

The four below are **facts about how the manuscript is written**. Neither this note nor the class speculates about why. Speculation kills the material immediately — see (7)(d) and (8).

**(1) The motel count appears as two values.** The warning block in Exhibit A lists where in the manuscript each value appears. The two values **are not split by stage but mixed across locations**.
→ What it asks: **which number will you cite** when you read this paper? And what do you commit to the moment you choose one? F-4 uses this material.

**(2) The observation count drops in two stages.** The rule the manuscript states is one sentence, quoted in Exhibit A. How the removed share was removed is not in the manuscript.
→ What it asks: **how far can a result travel when you do not know its denominator?** The second item of body §8 and item (3) of §9 use this material. If a student fills it in as "the motels that never reply were dropped wholesale", catch it there — the manuscript does not say that.

**(3) The rating-decline variable is defined two ways.** The discovery route in (2) opens this material.
→ What it asks: **the effect of what will you read that coefficient as?** Item (6) of body §9 picks it up.

**(4) One control variable's subscript and scope are stated two ways.** Both statements are quoted in Exhibit A.
→ What it asks: does this variable **take a different value per facility or the same value for all of them?** And depending on that answer, what does it share with the model's time fixed effects? Item (7) of body §9 sets this out as two questions. **There is no answer in this note either** — there is no raw data and the manuscript does not address it. What to observe in class is not the answer but **whether the student reaches the divergence.**

> **The sentence that runs through all four** — these are conditions handed to a reader, not verdicts on the manuscript. When a student tilts toward verdicts, send them back to (8).

## (4) Why the two original figures were not reprinted, and where students are sent instead

In Exhibits C and D, the place where the manuscript's figures sit carries **the caption, the axis labels, and one sentence on what the manuscript showed with that figure**. The figures themselves are absent.

**There are two reasons, and students are told both.**

- **Reprinting a figure is a rights question.** Numbers are facts and can be quoted; a figure is a work. Knowing that distinction while building teaching material is itself one of the things to learn in this course.
- **It builds the habit of opening the original paper.** This case is built to close on the Exhibits alone, and Supplement F sets no question that needs a figure. So going to look at the figure becomes **a choice rather than a duty**, and the students who make that choice separate from those who do not.

**What to tell students (readable as written)**

> "Two figures are missing from Exhibits C and D. The caption, the axis labels, and the sentence the manuscript showed with them are there. Today's work can be done without the figures. But if you want to see what the moderation actually looks like, open the original paper. The route is the citation and the DOI in the Exhibit set's preamble."

**How to get there** — the citation and DOI are in the Exhibit set's preamble. Putting the DOI straight into the address bar reaches the paper. A one-line note on the library route for students who need institutional access reduces drop-off.

**What to ask a student who went and looked** — does what the figure shows say the same thing as the one sentence written in the Exhibit? Does the figure tell you anything more, or does it show the same thing another way? **The body text does not answer this.** Only a student who looked can answer, which is what makes the trip worth it.

## (7) Four misreadings students commonly fall into — know them and wait

The four below are misreadings **this case invites**. It is built to invite them, so their appearance is normal; their absence usually means the student did not look at the tables. The body text handles all four, but **knowing them in advance and meeting them on the spot are different things.**

### (a) Reading a correlation coefficient as grounds for policy

**Symptom** — seeing the negative correlation in Table 3 of Exhibit E and concluding that replying faster reduces reviews.

**When it appears** — at the hand-off from §5 to §8, or while working F-2.

**Response** — do not rebut; **hand it back**: "What was that number measured across? And what does the coefficient in Table 4 hold fixed before it is computed?" Answering the two in order produces a self-correction. The fifth item of body §8 lays the same route out in sentences.

**Stop here** — the **mechanism** behind the two numbers pointing different ways is not something the manuscript interprets. If the instructor explains a mechanism, that explanation came from outside the manuscript. That is why body §8 leaves it open as a discussion item.

### (b) Concluding from a standalone coefficient in an interaction model

**Symptom** — seeing the non-significant standalone coefficient in Exhibit C and concluding that speed does not matter.

**Why it is common** — reading a table top to bottom puts the standalone coefficient first, and a row without significance marks reads as "no effect." **The reading order of the table is itself the trap.**

**Response** — do not deny the conclusion; **ask the condition**: "At what value of the other variable is that coefficient the effect?" Then: "Does that state exist in this data?" That is the order of F-3 parts 1 and 2.

**What to raise alongside** — two coefficients in that table share the same digits. If a student tries to **read meaning into it**, send them to F-3 part 4. Either judgment is fine, as long as they do not assert without material.

### (c) Turning a non-supported hypothesis into the opposite conclusion

**Symptom** — seeing the non-significant coefficient in Model 4 of Exhibit D and reading it as "replying fast in a week when the rating dropped actually hurts." The negative sign makes this one easier to fall into.

**Why it is dangerous** — (a) and (b) overstate the grounds, but **(c) manufactures grounds that are not there.** It is the most expensive kind in a practitioner report.

**Response** — put the standard error and the coefficient side by side. Then write two sentences apart on the board: "this prescription is wrong" / "this evidence cannot say this prescription." Body §7 fixes that distinction as the most important thing in the case, and F-5 part 3 asks the same thing.

**Do not miss** — this misreading also **arrives through AI summaries.** Item (5) of body §9 shows that route. If a student used a tool, (c) may be the tool's misreading rather than the student's, and whether the student knows the difference is the core of §9.

### (d) Settling two values as "one of them is a typo"

**Symptom** — seeing the motel count appear as two values and settling it as "one of them must be a typo", or "the earlier one is right and the later one slipped in." Then the next question arrives — **"So how many is it?"**

**Why it is a misreading** — the manuscript uses the two numbers **each in different places.** The warning block in Exhibit A lists those places. **Which one is correct is not settled by the manuscript alone.** The moment one of them is treated as a typo, something unsettled has been turned into something settled, and **the grounds for that judgment came from outside the manuscript.**

**⚠ If the instructor gives an answer, this material dies.** Name a number in reply to "so how many is it" and the student closes, in an instant, the spot this case most wants them to hold. And the next time they meet the same divergence in another paper, **they will wait for someone to settle it for them.** That is why this note does not name that number either.

**Response** — do not answer; **turn it into work**:

> "I do not know how many either, not from the manuscript alone. Let us do this instead — open Exhibit A and transcribe which value sits in which location, as a list."

Filling the list in shows that the two values **are not split by stage but mixed across locations**. Ask again at that point: "Looking at this list, can you say one of them is a typo? If you can, which one, and where in the list are your grounds?" That is F-4 parts 1 and 2, and item (1) of section (3) is the same material.

**Stop here** — do not speculate about **why** the two values are mixed. That a revision split them, or that a table was re-run, is all talk from outside the manuscript. If a student offers such a guess, take it but **set it aside as "something we cannot check."** In training to read a manuscript, separating what can be checked from what cannot comes before reaching a conclusion.

> **(d) and section (3) are a pair.** If (3) is the list of material, (d) is **how that material dies.** All four items in (3) carry the same risk — "one of them must be a slip" attaches to the two definitions in item (3) as readily as to the subscripts in item (4). The response is the same. Do not answer; turn it into a list.

## (8) What this case does not answer — and where the instructor holds the line

### First, what this case is

**This case is not material that criticizes the manuscript. It is material about how to read published research.**

The instructor has to know in advance that this distinction is structurally easy to lose. Half the session works on **things the manuscript does not settle** (the four in section (3), F-1 and F-4, items (6) and (7) of §9). The material is shaped that way, so left alone the air in the room tilts toward **"this paper is wrong."** Two things are lost when it tilts. Students learn fault-finding instead of reading, and that habit does not come back to help them when they write their own paper.

**Signs of the tilt — what the instructor should recognize**

- Questions in the register of "how did this paper get through review"
- A student presentation filled only with a list of findings, with nothing on **what can therefore be cited**
- One of the four in section (3) starting to be filled in with **speculation about causes** (see (7)(d))
- Conclusions converging on "these results cannot be trusted" — while the main effects in Table 4 hold consistently across the three models

**How to hold the line — four ways**

1. **Fix what this is at the start.** One sentence in the opening: *"What we are doing today is not reviewing this paper. It is practice in reading a published paper and making a decision on top of it. Published papers also have unsettled places, and recognizing them is today's skill."*
2. **Attach "so what can be cited" to every finding.** Each time a divergence is found, ask straight back: "Then what is left in this paper that you can cite?" Usually a great deal is left. That one question separates fault-finding from reading.
3. **Count what is consistent alongside.** The direction and magnitude of the main effects do not move across the three tables in Exhibits B, C and D. One control variable's coefficient is nearly the same value in all three. **Counting only what diverges and not what holds is itself a misreading.**
4. **Turn the same standard on the students.** At the end, ask: "If you held the report you are writing now to this standard, what happens?" When that question lands, the air changes.

> **Surfacing** a place the manuscript does not settle and **judging it a defect** are different acts. This case does only the first. That the Exhibits quote the divergences without resolving them, and that this note does not write down causes, are the same reason.

### The limitations the manuscript states for itself — §5.3

The manuscript states **four** limitations in §5.3. Below is the range the manuscript states, and this note does not go beyond it.

1. **Success was measured by review count.** Following prior work, review counts were used, but the manuscript writes that **there is a limit to measuring actual visitors precisely.** It adds that future work could use actual visitor counts or sales data.
2. **Customers who do not use the platform's information** — the manuscript writes that there is a limit to analyzing whether they actually made use of the host's response, and that **the customer reviews a seller can respond to were set in a limited way.**
3. **Facility-specific characteristics** — the manuscript writes that information on type, price and location was not collected. It adds that cumulative review count was used to account for characteristics such as recognition, to reduce that limit.
4. **The linguistic and content characteristics of reviews and responses were not reflected**, the manuscript writes. It adds that future work needs to bring in natural language processing.

**When a student asks "so if we reply more, do sales go up"** — this is where you stop. The answer is already inside the first item of 5.3. What the manuscript measured is review counts, and the manuscript itself writes that this does not measure visitors precisely. **Sales are not what this study observed.** So the answer is neither yes nor no but **"this study was not designed to answer that question."** And there is something to ask next — then what would Harujae have to observe in order to answer it? That question leads into the decision in body §10.

### What is not in 5.3 — keep this distinction

The four in section (3), and the identification questions in §9, are **not on the 5.3 list.** So they must **not be introduced as "limitations the manuscript acknowledges."** They are **facts** about how the manuscript is written, conditions a reader meets. The two are different in kind.

- **Limitations the manuscript acknowledges** — the authors drew the boundary themselves. The four above.
- **Facts as the manuscript is written** — the four in section (3). The authors did not address them, and this case does not address their causes either.

Mixing the two in class means students write down as acknowledged what the authors never acknowledged. **It is a question of citation accuracy, so it sits inside what this case teaches.**

### What this case does not take up

- **There is no raw data.** No reanalysis, and no re-estimation under other settings, happens in this case. That is also why the two questions in §9 have no answer.
- **Extension to other platforms or industries is not taken up.** What the sample is sits in Exhibit A, and generalization past that is not supported by this case.
- **Harujae's people, firm and branches are teaching-purpose synthesis.** The body preamble states it. So that students do not mix the synthesis with the figures that came from the manuscript, confirm once each time a table comes up that its source is an Exhibit.
